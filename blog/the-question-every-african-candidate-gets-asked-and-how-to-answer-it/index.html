The Question Every African Candidate Gets Asked — And How to Answer It Well

"How do we know you can deliver from there?" It is usually asked more politely than that, and it is really three worries in one. Answer all three.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 16 July 2026 5 min read

It arrives in different wrappers. "How is your internet?" "How do you handle the time difference?" "Have you worked with an international team before?" "What is your setup like?"

Underneath, the interviewer is asking one thing: if I hire you, will I regret it? And they are worried about three specific things. Answer all three and the question stops being an obstacle.

Worry one: infrastructure

Will the internet fail during a client call? Will the power go out mid-shift?

Do not say: "My internet is fine, no problem at all." Everyone says that, and it sounds like it has not been thought about.

Say instead: "I run two connections from different providers, so if one drops the other takes over. I also have an inverter that covers about six hours, and if both failed I would move to a co-working space fifteen minutes away. In two years I have missed one meeting and I gave notice forty minutes ahead of it."

Specific beats reassuring. They are not asking whether problems exist. They are asking whether you have already planned for them.

Worry two: overlap

Will you be awake when the team needs you?

Know the actual maths and say it out loud. West Africa is one hour ahead of UK time, so a European team is almost fully overlapping. US Eastern is five hours behind, so their 9am is your 2pm — you overlap all their morning. US Pacific is eight hours behind, which is genuinely harder.

Say: "Your team starts at 9am Eastern, which is 2pm for me, so we overlap from 2pm until about 7pm my time every day. I can start earlier or later if there is a specific reason. What are the hours you actually need someone live?"

That last question is the strong part. It moves you from being examined to solving the problem together.

Worry three: communication

Will managing you take more effort than it saves?

This is the biggest one and it is rarely stated. The answer is not words, it is evidence: how clearly you have written to them so far, how well-structured your answers are, whether you send a short recap after the call.

Say: "I work written-first. I send a short end-of-day note with what moved, what is blocked and what I need from anyone. That way nobody has to schedule a meeting to find out where things are."

Then actually do it after the interview. Send a four-line recap that evening. You have now demonstrated the claim instead of making it.

The reframe underneath all of it

You are not asking them to overlook where you are. You are a professional who has already solved the logistics, which is more than most candidates anywhere have done. Answer from that position and the question loses its weight.
